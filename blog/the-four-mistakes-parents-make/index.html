The Four Mistakes Every AAU Parent Makes

The short answer: parents hurt their kid's development most by critiquing what they don't understand, grading every game in the car ride home, pressuring performance until the joy disappears, and judging a whole season by one bad night. Fix these four and almost everything else gets easier.

Coach Dennis Kane has spent 44 years on the bench, 440 wins, 71 players placed in college basketball, and in that time he's watched more good kids get worse from home than from any bad coach or bad call. Not from bad intentions. From a lack of knowledge, unrealistic expectations, and undue pressure that parents don't realize they're applying. Here are the four mistakes he sees most often.

1. Critiquing what they don't understand

Most parents never played organized basketball at a high level, and even the ones who did are decades removed from how the game is actually taught now. That doesn't stop them from coaching from the stands or the car. Coach Kane is direct about this one: "Their job is to love, support, and encourage, not teach the game." Technical correction is the coach's job. A parent second-guessing footwork or shot selection without the training to actually evaluate it does more damage than good, and it teaches a kid that home isn't a safe place to fail.

2. Evaluating their child's play after a game

The car ride home is the single most damaging fifteen minutes in youth sports. A kid who just played their heart out gets a play-by-play breakdown of everything they did wrong, from a parent who means well and is making it worse. Coach Kane's rule here is simple and absolute: "They should never do that." The job after a game is to be glad to see them, not to run game film.

3. Taking the joy out of the game

Pressure to perform, tied to scholarships, roster spots, or a parent's own identity, replaces the reason most kids started playing in the first place. Once basketball becomes a source of anxiety instead of a source of joy, effort and improvement both suffer, and burnout becomes a real risk regardless of talent.

4. Placing games over development

One bad shooting night gets treated as the whole truth about a kid's game, and one good night gets treated as proof they've arrived. Neither is true. Coach Kane's core principle: "Development is something over time. Games are just a snapshot of what they know and what they've learned." Judging a season by its highlight reel or its worst night misses what's actually happening underneath.

What to do instead

None of this means disengaging. It means changing the job description, from coach and critic to support and encouragement, and trusting a real development plan instead of a single game's result.
