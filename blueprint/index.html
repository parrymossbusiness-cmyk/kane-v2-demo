The Car Ride Home

The drive home isn't your coaching moment.

Twenty years from now your child won't remember the score. They'll remember whether you were their biggest supporter or their harshest critic. The drive home is where that memory gets made.

Coach Kane's core principle

“If they want to talk, listen. If they don't, let them process.”

The first five minutes

Before you leave the parking lot

Your job: set the tone for the whole night in one sentence.

  • Your first words: “I love watching you play.” Nothing else.
  • Don't bring up the game. They decide if and when to talk.
  • If they're quiet, let them be quiet. Silence is not a problem to fix.
  • Never criticize the coach, the referee, or a teammate in the car. Your child hears “not good enough.”

When they do want to talk

Listen first

Emotions are indicators, not dictators.

  • Ask “How did you feel out there?” before anything about points.
  • Ask the questions a coach asks: “Did we play hard? Did we do what we were told?”
  • Let them name their own mistakes before you name any.
  • Point out one effort play (a loose ball, a box-out, a sprint back) over any score.

What to say instead

“Why didn't you shoot?”

“I loved how hard you ran back.”

“That ref cost you the game.”

“What do you think you learned tonight?”

“We need to talk about your defense.”

“Want to grab something to eat?”

Playing Time

Three things decide minutes. Talent is third.

Every parent has asked it: “Why isn't my kid playing more?” In 44 years of coaching, the answer almost always came down to the same three things. Two of them are completely in your child's control.

Coach Kane's core principle

“Playing time is earned, not given.”

Number one

Doing what you're told

Coaches play the kids they trust to run the plan. That trust is built in practice, not in games.

Ask after games: “Did we do what we were told?”

Number two

Playing hard

Sprinting back, loose balls, box-outs, every possession, at both ends of the floor. Coaches notice it before anything else.

Praise this: the effort plays, more than the points.

Number three

Talent

It grows, but it takes years of focused, correct repetition. Practice wrong and you get better at wrong.

Remember: fifteen focused minutes beat two hours of messing around.

How to talk about minutes

Who should ask the coach

The conversation that earns playing time is between the player and the coach. Not the parent and the coach.

  • Your child asks: “Coach, what do I need to improve to earn more minutes?”
  • If a parent has to step in, use the 24-hour rule: wait a full day after you're upset, then ask for a conversation.
  • Never before or after a game. Never by text in the heat of the moment.
  • The word to use is development, not minutes. Ask what your child needs to develop, and the coach hears a partner instead of a complaint.

When minutes are low

How to read your kid's minutes

Low minutes are not always a problem. Here is how I read them.

  • A quarter to half the game is normal. If it's less than that, you hope they're still real minutes, and your kid asks the coach what to work on.
  • Under five minutes a game over several tournaments is different. That's too much bench time, and it may not be developing your kid.
  • That's when you ask for a private meeting. Not after the game. Not on the sideline.
  • If your kid is stuck at the end of the bench, your reaction at home decides whether they quit or push through.

What to say instead

“The coach has favorites.”

“What did Coach say you need to work on?”

“I'm going to talk to the coach.”

“You ask Coach. I'll help you practice it.”

Mental Toughness

Teach them to talk to themselves, not listen to themselves.

Your child's biggest opponent isn't on the other team. It's the voice in their head after a mistake. Listening hears “don't miss again.” Talking says “next play.” Here are the four checks that train that switch at home.

Check one

Self-talk

  • My child has a 3-second reset after a mistake (a breath, a clap, one word) and uses it in games.
  • “I can't” gets replaced with “How can I?” at home, not just at practice.

Check two

Breathing

  • They know the box breath: in for 4, hold 4, out for 4, hold 4.
  • Their free-throw routine starts with one breath, every time.

Check three

Process over outcome

  • Our post-game questions are about effort and execution, not points.
  • They can name the three things they control: effort, attitude, response.

Check four

Pressure vs. pleasure

  • I never let the pressure exceed the pleasure.
  • I watch for the warning signs: dreading practice, pre-game anxiety, a lost spark, falling apart after mistakes. When I see one, I reduce pressure. I don't push harder.

What to say instead

“Don't miss this one.”

“Breathe. Same routine.”

“Why did you do that?”

“What's the next play?”

“You have to win today.”

“Compete hard and have fun.”

One last thing

Your child will forget most of the scores this season. He will not forget what your face looked like when he came off the floor, or what the car felt like on the way home.

That part is entirely yours. It's also the part that's still working on him twenty years from now.
