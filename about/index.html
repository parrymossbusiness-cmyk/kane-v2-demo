Dennis Kane retired as the 6th winningest boys basketball coach in San Diego high school history: 440 wins, 6 CIF finals appearances, 4 state playoff bids, and 1 CIF title, across a coaching career that started when he was 14 years old. He is 71 today, and he has spent almost his entire life on a bench, in a gym, or on a phone with a parent trying to understand what's actually happening with their kid.

In his own words

Why I coach parents now

"I built my career on turning losing programs into winning ones. Not with talent I didn't have, but by inspiring desire — professionalism, preparation, knowledge, and expertise, and the ability to get kids to buy in and sell out to something bigger than themselves. That's the whole method. It's not complicated, but it takes fifty years to get good at it."

"What changed my direction wasn't the players. It was the parents. I watched good kids get worse because of what was happening at home, not out of bad intentions, but out of a lack of knowledge, unrealistic expectations, and undue pressure parents didn't even know they were putting on their own kids. I saw it damage kids who had everything they needed to succeed."

"So here's what I actually do now. I build a plan to help your child reach their fullest potential, and I walk you through how to be alongside them on that journey, not in front of them, not behind them, alongside them. That's the job. It always was."

"And here's the part parents don't expect to hear: the players I'm proudest of aren't just the ones who played college ball. They're the ones building real lives now, in the Army, in corporate jobs, at companies like Meta, in careers that have nothing to do with basketball. That's the actual return on this. Basketball was never the whole point. It was how we got there."

The four mistakes

The four mistakes Coach Kane watches parents make

These are the patterns Dennis Kane sees most often, and the ones the Playbooks and the Audit are built to fix.

  • 01

    Critiquing what they don't understand

    Parents step into the role of coach without the knowledge, technique, or context to do it well.

    "Their job is to love, support, and encourage, not teach the game."
  • 02

    Evaluating their child's play after a game

    The car ride home becomes a film session the kid never asked for.

    "They should never do that."
  • 03

    Taking the joy out of the game

    Pressure to perform replaces the reason the kid started playing in the first place.

  • 04

    Placing games over development

    One bad shooting night gets treated as the whole truth about a kid's game.

    "Development is something over time. Games are just a snapshot of what they know and what they've learned."
